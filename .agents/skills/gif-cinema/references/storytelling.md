# Direct a sequence, not an extended loop

## Build the story from changes

Write the ending first, then the change that makes it possible. For a mini cartoon,
use a setup, a want/problem, an attempt, a consequence and a payoff as useful roles,
not a compulsory shot count. One beat can require several short shots. For a promo,
make the character's action reveal or transform the product instead of merely posing
beside text. For a motion-design brief, graphic elements themselves can be the actors.

For each shot record:

- **Before:** exact pose, prop owner/count, placement and contact.
- **Action:** something visibly changes: reach, catch, open, assemble, discover,
  react, offer. Include preparation, contact and settling when needed.
- **After:** state that the next shot can inherit, and a readable result hold.
- **Camera:** wide/medium/close, angle, horizon, screen direction, framing anchors.
- **Story purpose:** what the viewer understands now that they did not before.
- **Execution:** accepted source, missing character animation, local graphic, or
  available compositor. Identify references and which shot depends on an endpoint.

A target 30–60 seconds often needs many short actions. Estimate using actual drawing
counts and frame delays, then refine from decoded takes. Do not pad a seven-second
exchange into a minute with loops, a long introduction or slow holds. For a continuous
walk, repeated gait can be purposeful; label it as repeated motion, not new events.

## Visual bible and state ledger

Identity anchors should be visible and repeatable: silhouette, face proportions,
markings, costume construction, accent color, material and relative size. Include
negative constraints when concrete: one red scarf, no hat, two ears, one lantern.
Do not turn a new visual style into a fixed requirement for all future projects.

Keep a state ledger alongside the shots:

| Moment | Character pose | Prop owner/count | Contact and position | Next required state |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before catch | Hands apart, eyes on parcel | One parcel in flight | Parcel above palms | Fingers support parcel |
| After catch | Elbows bend with impact | One parcel held | Bottom rests on both palms | Keep parcel in next shot |

Track occlusion and causal order, not just approximate color. A box cannot open
before it is touched; a cup cannot be duplicated when handed over. Choose whether a
join is a continuous action, a deliberate angle change, an insert, or a time jump.
If the footage does not support a continuous join, disclose the discontinuity rather
than calling a dissolve a continuity repair.

## Prompt and shot design

Give each generated GIF one major action it can read in 2–16 drawings. Break an
entire chase or assembly into smaller causal steps. Example:

> The small cream fox, same teal scarf and triangular ears, lifts the single brass
> lantern from the ground with both paws. Preparation: bend knees and lower paws.
> Contact: both paws grip its handle. Result: stand with the lantern hanging below
> chest height. Locked medium three-quarter view; keep the whole lantern visible.

Opening/closing targets belong in the runtime's dedicated fields. The helper adds
camera, state and shared bible to its generated-shot inputs; it does not understand
or verify their visual meaning. When references are provided, describe their roles:
clean identity versus accepted previous endpoint versus environment.

For precise text and logos, compose approved artwork locally. Do not require the
character generator to spell them. Use a separate drawing or a real compositor for
layered prop motion; sliding a whole image cannot create a missing grasp or walk.

## Plan review

Read the action list with all captions hidden. Does the sequence still tell the
story? Do consequences follow triggers? Does the ending differ meaningfully from
the start? Does each required action have real footage or an explicit missing-asset
entry? Preview one difficult action and one difficult join before a large paid batch.
